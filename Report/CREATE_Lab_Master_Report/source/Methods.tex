\section{Methods}

\subsection{RL Formulation}
We consider a bimanual dexterous manipulation task in which a pair of robotic hands
$\mathbf{d} = \{d_l, d_r\}$ must reproduce a manipulation demonstrated by human hands
$\mathbf{h} = \{h_l, h_r\}$ interacting with two objects $\mathbf{o} = \{o_l, o_r\}$.
In our target task, the left hand stabilizes an alcohol bottle while the right hand picks
up and replaces the cap.
 
A human demonstration provides two reference signals: a hand trajectory
$\mathcal{T}^h = \{\tau^t_h\}_{t=1}^{T}$ encoding the wrist 6-DoF pose, linear and angular
velocity, and finger joint positions; and an object trajectory
$\mathcal{T}^o = \{\tau^t_o\}_{t=1}^{T}$ encoding the 6-DoF object's pose and velocity.
All translations are expressed relative to the dexterous hand wrist position to reduce
spatial complexity while preserving the direction of gravity.
 
We model the task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
$\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$.
The action for each hand at time $t$ comprises target joint positions
$a^t_q \in \mathbb{R}^K$ for proportional-derivative (PD) control of the fingers, and a
6-DoF wrench $a^t_w \in \mathbb{R}^6$ applied directly to the robotic wrist.

\subsection{Two-Stage Policy Architecture}
\label{sec:two-stage}
 
Our approach builds on the ManipTrans framework~\cite{maniptrans}, which separates the
manipulation transfer problem into two sequential stages: a pre-trained trajectory
imitation module and a residual correction module.
We adopt this architecture and describe it here for completeness. The difference lies in how we use it: instead of fitting a separate policy to each individual demonstration, we train a single shared policy across a set of demonstrations, as shown in \Cref{fig:multimaniptransdiagram}.


\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.24]{../figures/multimaniptransarchitecture.png}
    \caption{Our system architecture. We use a hand motion imitation model pre-trained on large-scale human demonstrations, then train a residual policy on top of it to adapt to a multi-demonstration task.}
    \label{fig:multimaniptransdiagram}
\end{figure}

 
\paragraph{Stage 1 — Hand Trajectory Imitator Policy}
The imitator $\pi_I$ is a pre-trained generalist policy that drives each robotic hand to
track the reference human hand trajectory without any object interaction.
Its state at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    s^t_I = \bigl\{\tau^t_h,\; s^t_\mathrm{prop}\bigr\},
    \qquad
    s^t_\mathrm{prop} = \bigl\{q^t_d,\; \dot{q}^t_d,\; w^t_d,\; \dot{w}^t_d\bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $q^t_d$ and $w^t_d$ denote the finger joint angles and wrist pose respectively,
together with their velocities.
At each step the imitator samples an action
$a^t_I \sim \pi_I\!\left(a^t \mid s^t_I,\, a^{t-1}\right)$.
 
\paragraph{Stage 2 — Residual Policy.}
A residual module $\pi_R$ refines the imitator's output to satisfy the task-specific
physical constraints arising from object interaction.
Its state $s^t_R = s^t_I \cup s^t_\mathrm{interact}$ augments the imitation state with
object-related information \begin{equation}
    s^t_\mathrm{interact} = \bigl\{
        \tau^t_o,\; p^t_{\hat{o}},\; \dot{p}^t_{\hat{o}},\;
        m_{\hat{o}},\; G_{\hat{o}},\; \mathrm{BPS}(\hat{o}),\;
        D(j^t_d, p^t_{\hat{o}}),\; C^t
    \bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $p^t_{\hat{o}}$ and $\dot{p}^t_{\hat{o}}$ are
the simulated object position (relative to the wrist) and velocity, $m_{\hat{o}}$ and
$G_{\hat{o}}$ are the object center of mass and gravitational force vector,
$\mathrm{BPS}(\hat{o})$ is a Basis Point Set encoding~\cite{bps} of the object shape,
$D(j^t_d, p^t_{\hat{o}})$ is the squared Euclidean distance from each hand keypoint to
the object, and $C^t$ is the contact force at each fingertip obtained from the simulator.
 
At each step, the residual correction
$\Delta a^t_R \sim \pi_R\!\left(\Delta a^t \mid s^t_R,\, a^t_I,\, a^{t-1}\right)$
is added element-wise to the imitator action, giving $a^t = a^t_I + \Delta a^t_R$,
clipped to the joint limits of the hand.
Because the imitator already provides a reasonable motion prior, the residual corrections
are expected to be near zero at the start of training.
We follow~\cite{maniptrans} in initializing the residual module with a zero-mean Gaussian
weight distribution and applying a linear warm-up schedule to its training, which prevents
collapse and accelerates convergence.
During training, the imitator weights are frozen; only the residual policy is updated.

\subsection{Reward Design}
\label{sec:reward}
 
\paragraph{Imitator reward.}
We use the pre-trained imitator provided by ManipTrans~\cite{maniptrans} directly and do
not retrain it.
For reference, its reward combines wrist tracking, finger keypoint tracking, and motion
smoothness:
\begin{equation}
    r^t_I =
    w_\mathrm{wrist} \cdot r^t_\mathrm{wrist} +
    w_\mathrm{finger} \cdot r^t_\mathrm{finger} +
    w_\mathrm{smooth} \cdot r^t_\mathrm{smooth}.
\end{equation}
The finger imitation term takes the form
\begin{equation}
    r^t_\mathrm{finger} =
    \sum_{f=1}^{F}
    w_f \cdot \exp\!\Bigl(-\lambda_f \,\bigl\|j^t_{d_f} - j^t_{h_f}\bigr\|_2^2\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $F = 21$ keypoints are selected on each robotic hand to correspond to the MANO
model~\cite{mano}, and weights $w_f$ and decay rates $\lambda_f$ are set to emphasize the
fingertips of the thumb, index, and middle fingers.
 
\paragraph{Residual reward.}
For the residual stage we extend the imitation reward with two object-centric terms:
\begin{equation}
    r^t_R =
    r^t_I +
    w_\mathrm{object} \cdot r^t_\mathrm{object} +
    w_\mathrm{contact} \cdot r^t_\mathrm{contact}.
\end{equation}
The \emph{object following} reward $r^t_\mathrm{object}$ penalizes positional and velocity
deviation of the simulated object from its reference trajectory.
The \emph{contact} reward $r^t_\mathrm{contact}$ encourages appropriate fingertip forces
whenever the reference hand-to-object distance falls below a threshold $\xi_c$:
\begin{equation}
    r^t_\mathrm{contact} =
    w_c \cdot \exp\!\left(
        \frac{-\lambda_c}
             {\displaystyle\sum_{f=1}^{F}
              C^t_{d_f} \cdot
              \mathbf{1}\!\left(D\!\left(j^t_{h_f},\, p^t_o \cdot o\right) < \xi_c\right)}
    \right),
\end{equation}
where $j^t_{h_f}$ is the position of the $f$-th human fingertip at time $t$ and $p^t_o$ is the
reference pose of object $o$. The product $p^t_o \cdot o$ denotes the object mesh $o$ rigidly
transformed by that pose, i.e.\ the object surface as it is posed at time $t$, and
$D(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the minimum Euclidean distance from a point to that surface. The indicator
$\mathbf{1}(\cdot)$ is therefore one exactly when the reference fingertip lies within $\xi_c$
of the object surface, so only fingertips that the demonstration places in contact contribute
to the sum. $C^t_{d_f}$ is the contact force measured at the corresponding fingertip of the
dexterous hand, $w_c$ scales the term and $\lambda_c$ sets its decay rate.
 
This reward formulation deliberately avoids task-specific engineering, keeping the signal
general across demonstrations — a property that is central to our multi-demonstration
generalization objective.
